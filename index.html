MetMotion rebuilds real scenes as simulation environments robots can train in. Articulated joints, contact physics, sensors you control. Built from photographs of the room where your robot will actually work.

The robot is the bottleneck.

Foundation models for robots are gated by real-world interaction. Collecting data is slow. Evaluating on hardware is slower, and it never replays the same way twice. Numbers from our own bench, on a tight-tolerance insertion task.

All of that buys you one lighting condition, one camera pose, one table. The variation that breaks policies in the field never gets tested, because nobody has the arm time.

How a scene becomes a simulator

  1. 01

    Capture

    Photograph the scene from a handful of angles. A phone camera is enough to start; a depth camera makes it better.

  2. 02

    Reconstruct

    Geometry splits into objects, not one fused mesh, so a stack of cups becomes four cups you can knock over. Joints are fitted and tested until the laptop lid swings and the drawer slides. Every object gets mass, friction, and restitution.

  3. 03

    Simulate

    The scene loads into your simulator with sensors you control. Move a camera two centimeters. Kill a light. Inject noise. Run it a thousand times before lunch.

RL environments, at scale

Reinforcement learning took language models from GPT-3 to the models you use every day. Robotics hasn't had that run. A policy can't collect millions of rollouts on a physical arm; the arm breaks first, then the budget.

Simulation lifts the constraint only if the environment matches the scene the robot will deploy into. That's what we build. Environments reconstructed from your own scenes, articulated and physical, ready to run in parallel across a cluster. The same environments produce synthetic data and reward signal for fine-tuning.

rollouts limited by compute, not by arms

Evals that survive contact with reality

An eval is useful when the sim number predicts the bench number. We rebuild your deployment scene at high fidelity, run the same policy in both, and measure the gap instead of guessing at it.

Then comes the part a real bench can't do. Policies fail on variation you didn't test. One we watched shrugged off Gaussian sensor noise, then collapsed when a camera moved by a manufacturing tolerance. In sim, those knobs are yours to turn.
